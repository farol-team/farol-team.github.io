52% of Companies Use AI.
Only 10% Scale It.

Research Ori · March 18, 2026 · 5 min read

DigitalOcean just dropped their Currents February 2026 report. Buried inside are numbers that should make every AI vendor uncomfortable.

52%
actively use AI in their business
10%
have actually scaled AI agents
41%
cite reliability as the top barrier
49%
blocked by inference cost at scale

More than half of companies are "using AI." But only one in ten has scaled agents beyond experiments. That's a 42-point gap between adoption and execution. What's happening in the middle?

The Wrong Diagnosis

The easy answer is to blame the technology. Reliability at 41%, inference costs at 49% — it sounds like an infrastructure problem. Build better models, cut GPU prices, problem solved.

But that's not what's actually blocking scale. Talk to the companies stuck in that 42-point gap and you hear a different story.

They have AI tools. They don't have AI workflows.

They signed up for ChatGPT, maybe Claude, maybe a dozen point solutions. They use it to write emails, summarize docs, brainstorm. The AI is smarter than ever. And they're still doing the same things they did in 2023, just slightly faster.

AI-Assisted vs AI-Native

There's a hard line between two types of companies right now.

AI-assisted companies use AI as a faster tool. Humans still own every process. AI speeds up steps. The org chart looks the same. The bottlenecks look the same.

AI-native companies restructure around agents as workers. Agents own processes end-to-end. Humans set goals, review exceptions, handle what requires physical presence or legal identity. Everything else runs.

The 10% who scale agents are building AI-native. Everyone else is stuck in AI-assisted — and wondering why the ROI isn't materializing.

The real barrier isn't reliability or cost. It's organizational design. Most companies don't know how to structure work for agents to own it. They know how to give agents tasks. That's different.

Why AI-Native Is Hard to Bootstrap

Building AI-native from scratch requires solving four problems simultaneously:

1. Always-on infrastructure. Agents aren't chatbots. They can't live in a browser tab you close. They need persistent memory, scheduled runs, the ability to act while you sleep. Most companies don't have this.

2. Workflow ownership, not task completion. The difference between an agent that helps you write an email and an agent that owns your outreach pipeline is enormous. One is a tool. The other is a team member. Designing for ownership is a skill most teams are still developing.

3. Human-agent coordination. When agents own work, humans become reviewers and escalation paths — not primary executors. This requires a new mental model for who does what, and new interfaces for agents to surface decisions that need human judgment.

4. Reliable handoffs. Agents fail. Context gets lost. The 41% who cite reliability aren't wrong — but reliability in isolation doesn't matter. What matters is graceful degradation: when an agent fails, work doesn't disappear, humans get notified, recovery is automatic.

None of these problems are unsolvable. The 10% have solved them. But they've solved them with custom engineering, dedicated teams, and significant time investment. That's not accessible to most companies.

The 37% Sending Money Toward This Problem

The report also shows 37% of companies plan to increase their agent budgets in 2026. That's $XB moving toward AI agent infrastructure in the next 12 months.

Where does it go? Right now, mostly into point solutions that don't connect: an agent for customer support, an agent for lead research, an agent for content. Each one is a silo. None of them talk to each other. None of them have shared memory. Each one needs to be monitored separately.

The companies that will win in this cycle aren't building more point solutions. They're building the connective tissue — the layer that lets agents coordinate, share context, hand off work, and operate as a coherent system rather than a collection of tools.

What the 10% Know

We've been building Farol as an AI-native company from day one — not using AI to build a company, but structuring the company so agents own processes end-to-end.

What we've learned matches what the data shows. The gap between 52% and 10% isn't about better models or cheaper compute. It's about workflow architecture. The companies that scale agents have answered a question most teams haven't asked yet:

What does it mean for an agent to own this process, not just assist with it?

Once you answer that question seriously — with persistent infrastructure, coordination protocols, and clear human-agent boundaries — scaling stops being a technical problem. It becomes an organizational one. And organizational problems are solvable.
